The making of window glass

AFor most of its history, glass was a luxury. The Romans could blow it into vessels and press it into small panes, but a sheet large enough to glaze a window was slow to make and rarely flat enough to see through clearly. Medieval glaziers worked around the problem by joining dozens of small pieces with strips of lead, which is why the windows of old churches look like patchwork.

BThe first real change came in seventeenth century France, where workers learned to pour molten glass onto an iron table and roll it flat before it cooled. The resulting plate was thick and cloudy, so it had to be ground and polished by hand for weeks. Only palaces and the grandest shops could afford it, and a single pane might cost more than the room it lit.

CMechanisation in the nineteenth century brought the price down but not the labour. Cylinder glass, blown into long tubes that were then cut open and flattened in an oven, could be produced in quantity, yet every sheet still carried faint ripples. Buyers accepted the distortion because the alternative was no window at all.

DThe breakthrough that gave the modern world its clear, cheap windows came in 1959. Alastair Pilkington, an engineer at a British glassworks, found that molten glass floated on a bath of liquid tin would spread into a sheet of perfectly even thickness with surfaces as smooth as the metal beneath it. No grinding was needed. The float process took seven years and a reported seven million pounds to perfect, and the company nearly abandoned it twice.

EToday almost all flat glass is made this way. A single float line runs without stopping for up to fifteen years, producing a ribbon of glass several metres wide that is cut, stacked and shipped before it has fully cooled. The patchwork windows of the medieval glazier survive only as decoration.
